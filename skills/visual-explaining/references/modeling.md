# Modeling

## Source coverage

Map substantive claims, relationships, conditions, examples, and exceptions in the requested material to visible explanations or reachable supporting detail. Keep source references alongside the corresponding content. Simplify presentation while preserving meaning; distinguish source claims from teaching assumptions and derived results.

## Visual model

A visual model represents the objects, relationships, properties, and rules needed to understand the source. Choose the representation around the question: dependencies, spatial structure, quantities, time, causality, or conceptual relationships.

Keep object identity and the meaning of visual encodings stable. Label relationships precisely; distinguish sequence, association, support, and causation. Make relevant states, action conditions, changed properties, and unchanged constraints observable.

## Interaction

For each important relationship or mechanism, consider how the learner could manipulate inputs, perform an action, compare cases, inspect a part, or move through time. Choose interactions that reveal the information with less interpretive effort. Retain a clear static explanation where interaction adds no useful observation.

## Explanation

Start from a familiar question and a reproducible initial situation. Keep the model central as explanation progresses: show what to notice, what happens, why it happens, and what the result helps explain. Connect new concepts to already introduced objects and relationships.

Where a prediction or explanation would reveal understanding, invite one before showing the answer. Retain the learner's reasoning and assistance received; a viewed animation or completed sequence does not establish understanding.
